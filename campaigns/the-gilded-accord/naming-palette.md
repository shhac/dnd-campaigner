# Naming Palette: The Gilded Accord

## Primary Palette: Creole (Mieville Constructed)

**Usage**: The dominant language of Môrivôs — a port city where tongues collided over centuries. Used for most character names, street names, districts, and everyday institutions.

**Phonetic Character**: Syllable-heavy, vowel-rich, with diacritical marks suggesting tonal stress. Hard consonants softened by trailing vowels. French/Portuguese/Haitian roots ground the constructed feel.

**Name Examples**:
- Elite/merchant class: Longer, multi-syllable, aspirational — Caudressô, Vélissande, Théodarrhe
- Street/working class: Clipped, punchy, worn smooth by daily use — Kes, Brûno, Tamô, Dolenne
- Places: Compound words, descriptive origins — Tidemarket, Caudressô House, the Pallisette

**Naming Conventions**:
- Surnames among the elite are compound (two roots joined): Caudressô, Ghalliverre, Thoraudenne
- Common folk often use a single name plus a district or trade: "Kes of Salthook" or "Brûno the Ledger"
- Diacritical marks (ô, é, û) signal old-money or institutional affiliation

## Secondary Palette: Euskaran (Basque)

**Usage**: The ancient language of the Accord itself, preserved in old-city institutions, legal terminology, and the Rampart's martial traditions. Names drawn from this palette feel archaic and formal.

**Phonetic Character**: Hard x/tx sounds, doubled vowels, terminal -ak/-ez/-itz endings. Angular and percussive compared to the flowing Creole.

**Name Examples**:
- Military/institutional: Goitxarri, Zumarratz, Aitzolenne
- Legal/ceremonial: Etxebarria, Galarreta, Zubeldia
- Places: Irrintzi Hall, the Mendizabal Steps

**Naming Conventions**:
- Rampart officers use patronymic chains: given name + clan name + post name (e.g., Goitxarri Zumarratz-Errialde)
- Old-city buildings and legal terms use Euskaran exclusively
- The Accord's formal name is Euskaran; its common name is Creole

## Palette Interactions

When Creole and Euskaran names appear together, it signals the layered history of Môrivôs — the old law underneath the living city. Characters with Euskaran names in a Creole-dominant world carry institutional weight or ancestral ties to the founding. Mixed names (one palette for given, another for surname) suggest cross-cultural heritage.
