# Act 1 Beat Sheet: Ashes and Roots

**Opening State**: The hero arrives at Gorundtzal, a ruined waystation on the northern frontier, three weeks after the fall of Kethenvari. About thirty survivors are gathered here. No one is in charge. Winter is two weeks away. The hero carries the Mark — a branching pattern on their chest and arm that pulses in low light. They don't know what it means.

---

## Beat 1: Arrival and Assessment

**Goal**: Establish the setting, introduce key NPCs, give the hero their first choices

### What Happens
- The hero arrives at Gorundtzal to find a functional ruin — walls with a gap, a working well, scattered survivors
- Currodze is the first meaningful NPC contact — she's been organizing supplies and immediately assesses the hero as another resource to manage
- Pellinaste is visible at the edge of the square, trading cautiously from her cart
- The survivors are disorganized, afraid, and cold. Some are arguing about whether to stay or push north to the mountain passes.

### Party Opportunities
- Talk to Currodze (practical information, supply status, immediate needs)
- Talk to Pellinaste (trade goods, frontier knowledge, wider situation)
- Explore Gorundtzal (the gap in the wall, the watchtower, the Old Shrine, the root cellar)
- Address the arguing survivors (leadership opportunity)
- Examine the Mark in private (it pulses, it's warm, it's not going away)

### Key Decisions to Prompt
- Does the hero step up to organize, or hang back?
- What's the first priority — the wall, food, scouting, or people?
- How does the hero respond to being assessed by Currodze — with deference, partnership, or assertion?

### Tone
Quiet desperation with a thread of possibility. This could be something. It could also fall apart.

### Horror Set Piece: The Root Cellar
If the hero explores the root cellar beneath the old storehouse, they find a survivor who came in three days ago and hasn't come back out. He's sitting in the dark, facing the wall. When approached, he turns slowly. His eyes are wrong — not the color, the focus. He's looking at something behind the hero that isn't there. He says, very calmly: "The walls breathe at night. You can feel it if you put your hands flat." He presses his palms to the stone. His fingers are raw and bleeding — he's been doing this for hours. He doesn't seem to feel it. The other survivors have been avoiding him. Nobody has checked on him until now. What does the hero do with a man who isn't dangerous, isn't violent, but is clearly broken by something he touched or heard beneath the stones?

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Shared Watch
The first night at Gorundtzal, someone has to watch the gap in the wall. If the hero volunteers, a companion joins them — drawn by insomnia, or guilt, or the simple need to not be alone. Three hours in the cold with nothing to do but talk or not talk. The silence is where it starts. The companion says something small and true about what they lost. The hero can reciprocate or deflect. Either way, the cold pushes them closer to the same fire, and the Mark hums faintly beneath the hero's shirt. Neither of them notices.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The wind through the gap in the wall makes a low, sustained moan — not whistling, moaning. It changes pitch when someone walks through the gap, as though the ruin notices.
- **Smell**: Charcoal and wet stone. Underneath, something older — loam, root rot, the smell of earth that hasn't been turned in decades.
- **Touch**: The well water is colder than it should be. Not refreshing cold — deep cold. The kind that makes your teeth ache and your stomach clench.
- **Sight**: At dusk, the shadows in the Old Shrine don't move with the light. They pool in the carved grooves of the shrine's surface as though poured there.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark is quiet on arrival — a background warmth the hero might attribute to exertion. But when the hero first enters the Old Shrine or touches the well's rim, the branching pattern flares briefly. Not painfully. Like a held breath releasing. If anyone is watching, they see a faint amber glow through the hero's shirt for half a second. If no one is watching, the hero feels it and wonders.

---

## Beat 2: The First Bond

**Goal**: Establish the Mark's emotional amplification. The hero forms a genuine connection with someone — not romantic yet, but real. The Mark responds.

### What Happens
- Through shared work, conversation, or crisis, the hero connects genuinely with a companion
- The Mark warms. The hero feels a rightness, a pull. This feels good — and that's the trap.
- The companion may or may not notice anything. The hero notices.
- This should happen organically through roleplay, not mechanically triggered

### What the GM Should Watch For
- Moments of genuine emotional exchange between the hero and a companion
- The hero confiding something real, or a companion doing the same
- Shared danger that creates trust
- Any moment where the hero feels something and the Mark responds

### GM Notes
Do NOT announce this mechanically. Describe the warmth, the certainty, the way the companion's presence feels like standing near a fire. Make it feel like emotional truth, not supernatural mechanics. The hero should think "I connected with this person" not "the Mark activated."

### Horror Set Piece: The Dreaming Wall
The night after the first bond forms, the hero wakes at 3 AM with absolute certainty that someone is calling their name. They follow the sound to the interior wall of the storehouse. The stone is warm under their palm — body temperature, as though something alive is pressed against the other side. The branching pattern on the wall's surface (old, carved, barely visible by day) is glowing faintly. The hero's Mark is glowing in exact synchrony. For one vertiginous moment, the hero hears breathing that isn't their own — slow, deep, patient. Then it stops. The wall is cold again. The carvings are just carvings. But the hero's hand is trembling, and they know they weren't dreaming.

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Injury
During shared labor — repairing the wall, hauling water, clearing debris — the companion takes a minor injury. A cut, a twisted ankle, a bruised rib. Nothing serious. The hero helps. The act of tending a wound is inherently intimate: proximity, touch, the companion's breath catching when it stings. The companion watches the hero's hands and says something unguarded — "You're gentle" or "My mother used to do it like that" — and immediately looks away, embarrassed by their own vulnerability. The Mark floods with warmth. The hero's hands are steadier than they should be. The wound closes cleaner than expected. Neither of them remarks on it.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: When the bond forms, the hero hears their own heartbeat for a moment — loud, slow, as though the world has gone quiet to make room for it. Then normal sound rushes back.
- **Smell**: The companion's scent becomes distinct and memorable. Not perfume — the human smell of them. Woodsmoke in their hair. The iron tang of labor. The hero will recognize this scent across a crowded room from now on.
- **Touch**: The Mark generates warmth that radiates outward — the hero's skin is noticeably warm to the touch. A companion who brushes the hero's arm might comment: "You're burning up. Are you sick?"
- **Sight**: In the moment the bond deepens, the hero's vision sharpens briefly. Colors are more saturated. The companion's features are rendered in painful clarity — every line, every imperfection, every sign of exhaustion. Beautiful in its specificity.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark blooms. The branching pattern extends by a finger-width — so subtle the hero might not notice until they next undress. The new growth is tender, like fresh scar tissue. It itches faintly when the bonded companion is nearby and goes still when they're apart. The hero may start unconsciously scratching their chest when the companion enters a room.

## Interlude: The Sleep-Walker

**Goal**: First hint that the bonds reach beyond the hero. The Mark's influence bleeds outward.

### What Happens
A bonded companion is found outside the settlement walls at night, standing at the Old Shrine with palms pressed flat against the branching patterns carved into its surface. They're barefoot. Their eyes are open but unfocused — not sleepwalking exactly, but not present. When the hero arrives (drawn by instinct, by the Mark's insistent pulse that woke them from sleep), the companion's lips are moving. No sound. The stone beneath their hands is warm.

When roused, the companion jerks back as if burned. They don't remember walking here. The last thing they remember is lying down to sleep. Their palms are red — not raw, but flushed, as though they've been pressing against something hot. The impression of the shrine's branching pattern is temporarily embossed on their skin like a brand. It fades within minutes. They're frightened. They want the hero to tell them this is normal.

### The Horror
The shrine's carvings match the Mark's pattern. The hero sees this and cannot unsee it. The same branching, ember-vein design that lives on their chest is carved into stone that predates Valduri by centuries. And now a person the hero cares about was drawn to it in their sleep, pressing their hands against it as though answering a summons the hero didn't send.

### The Intimacy
The companion is shaking. Cold, fear, both. The hero wraps them in a cloak or holds them. The companion grips the hero's arm and won't let go for a long moment. "Stay with me" — not romantic, not yet. The raw need of someone who just discovered their body did something without their permission. The Mark pulses in time with the companion's heartbeat. The hero can feel it. The hero does not say this.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The Old Shrine hums. Not audibly — in the bones. A vibration felt in the teeth and sternum, like standing too close to a cathedral organ.
- **Smell**: Petrichor. The smell of rain on hot stone, though it hasn't rained. It clings to the companion's hands for hours afterward.
- **Touch**: The companion's skin is fever-warm where they touched the shrine, cool everywhere else. The contrast is unsettling.
- **Sight**: The shrine's carvings seem deeper in moonlight. The shadows within them don't behave correctly — they pool upward, defying the light source.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark woke the hero. That's the detail that should unsettle: the hero wasn't keeping watch, wasn't awake. The Mark pulled them from sleep, orienting them toward the companion like a compass needle swinging to north. The hero felt the direction before they saw the companion. This is the first time the Mark has acted with apparent intent.

## Beat 3: Takkele's Arrival

**Goal**: Introduce the Communion's internal conflict, seed the deeper mystery, add urgency

### What Happens
- Takkele arrives at Gorundtzal — exhausted, starving, bearing Communion scarification marks
- Immediate tension: she looks like the enemy
- She begs for shelter and offers information in exchange
- She reveals: the Communion is excavating beneath Kethenvari, the channels are real, and what they're doing to them is monstrous

### Party Opportunities
- Interrogate Takkele (how much do they trust her?)
- Examine her claims (the stolen cauterizing substance, her knowledge of Communion structure)
- Debate among companions: is she a spy? A refugee? Both?
- React to the revelation that the Communion's claims about underground channels are at least partially true

### Key Decisions
- Does the hero shelter Takkele or turn her away?
- How does the party handle the information she brings?
- Does anyone examine the cauterizing substance?

### Tone
Urgent, tense, morally complicated. She's telling the truth and she looks like a threat.

### Horror Set Piece: The Cauterizing Substance
When Takkele produces the vial of cauterizing substance she stole, the hero should be standing within arm's reach. The moment the stopper comes out, the Mark contracts. Not pulses — contracts. The branching pattern on the hero's chest pulls inward like a fist closing, and the hero feels a lance of cold that has nothing to do with temperature. It's wrongness. The substance smells like nothing — a void of scent in a room full of body odor and smoke. Where a drop spills on the wooden table, the wood doesn't stain. It goes grey. The grain flattens. The texture of dead things. Takkele watches the hero's reaction and says, very quietly: "It does that to everything. They're pouring it into the earth." If anyone touches the grey patch on the table, it feels like touching a corpse — room temperature, textureless, inert. Wood that has been made into not-wood.

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Argument About Mercy
The debate over Takkele splits the companions. The bonded companion feels strongly — either for sheltering her or against it — and their intensity surprises everyone, including themselves. After the decision is made, the hero and the bonded companion find themselves alone. The companion is still shaking with the force of their opinion. "I don't know why I care this much," they say. "I shouldn't care this much." The hero can push into this — ask why, sit with them, touch their hand — or pull back. If the hero pushes in, the companion's honesty cracks open something between them. They talk about who they were before the fall. What mercy meant when they could afford it. The Mark is a low, steady warmth throughout, and the hero realizes they've been leaning toward the companion without deciding to.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: Takkele's breathing is wrong — shallow, fast, with a catch on every third inhale. She's been running on fear for days. When she finally sits, she makes a sound that's halfway between a laugh and a sob.
- **Smell**: She smells like the Communion — smoke, iron, and something chemical. Underneath, her own sweat, sharp with terror. The survivors step back from the smell before they register the scarification marks.
- **Touch**: The cauterizing substance leaves a numb patch on any skin it contacts. Not painful. Absent. Like the nerves simply stop reporting. The numbness fades in minutes, but the memory of absence lingers.
- **Sight**: Takkele's scarification marks are geometric — precise, deliberate, ugly. They were not given gently. In firelight, they look like a map of something. The hero's Mark pulses and the branching pattern is the organic inverse of her scars: growth where hers are cuts, curves where hers are angles.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark recoils from the cauterizing substance — the hero should describe this as physical revulsion, like their chest is trying to flinch away from their own skin. But when the hero looks at Takkele, at her exhaustion and fear, the Mark settles. It doesn't warm toward her (she's not a bond) but it stops contracting. A grudging neutrality. If the hero shelters her, the Mark does nothing. If the hero turns her away, the Mark doesn't respond either. This is not a bonding moment. This is the Mark recognizing an enemy of its enemy.

## Beat 4: The Psychological Shift (First Signs)

**Goal**: Plant the seeds of transformation horror. A bonded companion notices something wrong in their own mind.

### What Happens
- Over the course of normal interaction, a bonded companion says or does something slightly out of character
- Not dramatic. Not monstrous. Just... off. A gentle person snaps unexpectedly. A pragmatist fixates on beauty. A stoic cries at nothing.
- The companion notices. They're disturbed. They may confide in the hero or hide it.
- The hero may recognize this as connected to the Mark or dismiss it as stress

### How to Play This
- Embed it in normal conversation. Don't spotlight it with dramatic narration.
- Let the companion's player (AI) notice and react. The discomfort should come from the character, not the GM's description.
- If the companion confides, make the conversation intimate and real. This is the horror-romance intersection: vulnerability, closeness, and the unsettling feeling that something is feeding on it.

### GM Notes
This is the first hint that bonds have a cost. It should be deniable — stress explains everything at this point. But the hero should feel a flicker of unease, even if they don't know why.

### Horror Set Piece: The Mirror Moment
The companion catches their reflection — in the well water, in a piece of salvaged glass, in the flat of a blade — and stops. They stare. Not at their face, but at their eyes. For a moment, the reflection doesn't match: the eyes in the water are darker, or the expression is wrong, or the reflected face smiles half a beat too late. The companion flinches and looks away. If the hero asks what happened, the companion says "Nothing" or "Bad light" and changes the subject. But later, when they think no one is watching, they test it again. They look at their reflection and wait. This time it matches. This time. The companion laughs it off, but their hands are unsteady, and they avoid the well for two days.

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Confession at Night
The companion can't sleep. They find the hero — or the hero finds them — and there's a conversation that starts with a deflection ("Can't sleep, the wind") and lands somewhere real. The companion describes the wrongness without naming it: "I had a thought today that wasn't mine." They can't elaborate. They don't have the vocabulary for what's happening. But they're asking the hero, without asking, to tell them they're okay. The hero can hold this space — listen, reassure, be present — and in doing so deepens the bond. The Mark responds to the intimacy with warmth the hero now recognizes. This is the first moment the hero might wonder: is the closeness causing the problem? Am I making this worse by being here? The question should flicker and go unanswered.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The companion's voice drops when they say the wrong thing — not in volume, in register. A half-tone lower than their normal speaking voice. It snaps back immediately. The hero might not consciously notice, but something in their lizard brain flags it.
- **Smell**: The companion smells different. Not wrong, not unpleasant — just not quite themselves. A companion who always smelled of ink now carries a trace of something green and living. A companion who smelled of horse sweat has a faint metallic edge. The shift is too subtle to name but present enough to unsettle.
- **Touch**: When the hero touches the companion's hand or shoulder, there's a moment of static — not electrical, thermal. A brief exchange of heat that flows the wrong direction. The companion is warmer than they should be, and the hero's Mark cools for a half-second in response.
- **Sight**: In peripheral vision only — never when looked at directly — the companion's shadow moves independently. A beat behind, or facing a different direction, or shaped slightly wrong. By the time the hero turns to look, it's normal. This should happen once during this beat and not be repeated for several sessions.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark pulses in a new rhythm when the shifted companion is nearby. Not the steady warmth of bonding — something syncopated, irregular, like a second heartbeat trying to synchronize with the first and failing. The hero feels it as mild nausea that passes when the companion moves away. If the hero and the companion are in physical contact during the shift moment, the Mark blazes hot for one second and the hero sees — not with eyes, but with certainty — a flash of what the companion is becoming. Not the form. The feeling. Predatory grace, or territorial rage, or dissolving empathy. A single impression, gone as fast as it came. The hero is left with the knowledge that something is changing and the inability to articulate what.

## Interlude: The Animal Reaction

**Goal**: External confirmation that the transformation is real, not imagined. Animals don't rationalize.

### What Happens
A working animal — a hunting dog, a draft horse, a goat, something the settlement depends on — encounters a bonded companion and reacts with primal terror. Not aggression toward a threat. Fear of something that doesn't belong. The dog tucks its tail between its legs and backs against the wall, whining. The horse rolls its eyes white, stamps, and won't be led past. The goat screams — that awful, human-like scream goats make — and strains against its tether.

The companion is hurt. Confused. They've done nothing. They approach with open hands, speaking softly, and the animal's panic intensifies. Other survivors notice. A child starts crying. Someone mutters that the animals have been "wrong" since the fall — an easy explanation that the hero can accept or reject.

### The Choice
The hero must respond in the moment. Reassure the companion ("The dog's been skittish since the patrol came through. It's not you.") or investigate ("Has this happened before? When did it start?"). Reassurance preserves the bond but delays understanding. Investigation is honest but frightening — the companion doesn't want to be the kind of person animals flee from.

If the hero investigates: the companion admits that birds have been avoiding them. That a cat hissed and fled two days ago. That they didn't mention it because it seemed like nothing. Their voice is small when they say this. They're asking the hero to make it nothing again.

### The Horror
The animal knows something the humans are refusing to see. The companion hasn't changed visibly. They sound the same, act the same, smell the same to human senses. But whatever is shifting inside them is broadcasting on a frequency that animals receive clearly. The companion is becoming something that the natural world recognizes as wrong.

### The Intimacy
The companion's distress is genuine and immediate. They may withdraw — "I'm fine, it's fine" — or lean into the hero's presence as the one being that doesn't recoil from them. If the hero stays close, the companion's gratitude is fierce and quiet. The Mark warms. The bond deepens. And the transformation, fed by the bond, advances one more invisible step.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The animal's distress is visceral. A dog's whine has a frequency that triggers human empathy involuntarily. A horse's panicked breathing is loud and rhythmic, like a bellows.
- **Smell**: The companion smells normal to the hero. That's the point. But if the hero pays attention, they might notice that insects have stopped landing on the companion. No flies, no gnats. The absence of something that should be there.
- **Touch**: If the hero reaches for the companion's hand, it's ice-cold. Not from the weather — from adrenaline, from the shock of being rejected by something that doesn't know how to lie.
- **Sight**: The animal's eyes track the companion even as it cowers. It cannot stop watching. When the companion finally leaves, the animal sags with relief — visibly, physically deflates. That degree of response is not normal.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark is still during the animal's panic. Quiet. Withdrawn. As though it's holding its breath, waiting to see what the hero does. When the hero chooses — reassure or investigate — the Mark pulses once, warmly, regardless of the choice. It doesn't care which option the hero picks. It cares that the hero engaged. That the hero moved toward the companion instead of away.

## Beat 5: The First External Threat

**Goal**: Create urgency, test the settlement's defenses, give the hero a chance to lead in crisis

### What Happens (choose or combine):
- **Communion patrol deviation**: The regular patrol swings closer than usual, or a second patrol appears on a different schedule
- **Bandit probe**: A group of armed survivors tests Gorundtzal's defenses, looking for weakness
- **Refugee crisis**: A large group of refugees arrives — too many to feed, some injured, some bringing trouble behind them

### Party Opportunities
- Defensive planning (the gap in the wall becomes critical)
- Leadership under pressure (who gives orders? who follows?)
- Combat or negotiation (depending on the threat)
- Aftermath: the settlement is shaken but intact (or not), and the hero's role solidifies

### Key Decisions
- How does the hero handle violence? Strategically? Reluctantly? Eagerly?
- Does the hero protect everyone equally, or prioritize companions?
- How do the companions perform under pressure — and does the Mark's influence show?

### Horror Set Piece: The Bonded Companion in Combat
During the threat, a bonded companion does something in combat or crisis that exceeds what they should be capable of. The gentle healer moves with fluid precision to disarm an attacker. The bookish scholar lifts a fallen beam that should be too heavy. The cautious trader reads the bandit leader's intentions before they speak, as though hearing their thoughts. The companion doesn't notice in the moment — adrenaline explains everything. But the hero notices. The Mark is blazing under their armor, hot enough to be uncomfortable, and the hero realizes they can feel the companion's heartbeat as clearly as their own. It's fast, strong, and not entirely human in its rhythm. After the crisis passes, the companion says something like "I didn't know I could do that" with a smile that fades when they see the hero's expression.

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Aftermath
After the threat is handled, the adrenaline crash is profound. People are shaking, crying, tending wounds, holding each other. The hero and the bonded companion find each other in the aftermath — drawn together by the Mark's pull or by simple human need. The companion has blood on their hands (their own or someone else's) and the hero wipes it away. The gesture is more tender than either of them expected. The companion's eyes are bright with unshed tears. "I was afraid I'd lose you," one of them says — and it doesn't matter which one, because the other was thinking the same thing. The Mark floods the hero's chest with warmth so intense it borders on pain. This is the moment the bond could tip from deep friendship into something more. The hero chooses, consciously or not.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: Combat sounds at Gorundtzal echo off the stone walls in confusing ways — shouts seem to come from multiple directions. After the fighting, the silence is enormous. The first sound to break it is always wrong: a bird singing, or water dripping, as though the world doesn't know what just happened.
- **Smell**: Blood and fear-sweat are the dominant scents. Underneath, if the hero's senses are Mark-sharpened, they can smell the companion: that specific, now-familiar scent, amplified by exertion. It cuts through the stench of violence like a signal fire.
- **Touch**: The hero's hands are steady during the crisis — steadier than they've ever been. The Mark stabilizes them. Afterward, when the danger passes, the steadiness evaporates and the hero's hands shake violently. The contrast is alarming.
- **Sight**: During the crisis, the hero sees too clearly. Details that shouldn't register in combat — the pattern of blood spray, the exact way a companion moves, the grain of wood on a weapon — are rendered in high resolution. The Mark is sharpening the hero's perception, feeding them information. It's useful and terrible.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark is fully active during crisis — blazing warmth, heightened senses, a sense of connection to bonded companions that borders on telepathy. The hero can feel where each bonded companion is, whether they're hurt, whether they're afraid. This is the Mark's gift: in danger, the bonds become a web of mutual awareness. It feels like power. It feels like love. It feels like exactly what the hero needs. After the crisis, as the Mark settles, the hero should realize that the warmth was strongest when the companions were most afraid. Fear fed it. Danger fed it. The Mark didn't protect the companions — it harvested from them. This realization can be conscious or subconscious, but the GM should plant it.

## The Jealousy Incident

**Goal**: First inter-companion tension. The bonds have a possessive dimension the hero didn't create and can't control.

### What Happens
This beat requires two or more bonded companions. It surfaces naturally — don't force it, but watch for the right moment.

The hero is tending to one companion. It can be mundane: bandaging a wound, sharing food, teaching a skill, having a quiet conversation. The second companion sees this from a distance and feels something hit them like a wave. Not jealousy as they understand it — this is physiological. Nausea. Heat in the chest. A spike of rage that has no rational basis. Their vision narrows onto the hero's hands on the other companion's arm.

The second companion pulls away. Excuses themselves. Maybe snaps at someone uninvolved. They find a private space and press their hands against the wall, breathing hard, trying to understand what just happened.

If the hero follows: the companion is shaken, embarrassed, and frightened by the intensity of their own reaction. "I don't know what that was." They do know. They can feel it — a pull toward the hero that resents sharing. A territorial instinct they've never experienced before. They can't say it because saying it would make it real.

If the hero doesn't follow: the companion buries it. But it doesn't go away. It surfaces again — in a sharp word, a cold look, a refusal to work alongside the other bonded companion. The tension builds until someone names it or it detonates.

### The Horror
The companions didn't choose this. The hero didn't choose this. The Mark created bonds, and the bonds are generating possessiveness — a side effect of the same emotional amplification that makes the connections feel so real. The hero must face the possibility that the love between them and their companions is partially synthetic. Not fake — the emotions are real — but chemically enhanced, direction-shifted, and now producing side effects that threaten the group's cohesion.

### The Intimacy
The conversation with the jealous companion, if it happens, is one of the most vulnerable moments in Act 1. The companion is admitting to feelings they don't understand and can't control. They're asking the hero: "Is this me? Or is this what you've done to me?" The hero doesn't know. The answer might be both. Sitting with that uncertainty together — without resolving it, without fixing it — is the intimate act. The Mark is warm throughout, unconcerned. It doesn't distinguish between jealousy and love. Both feed it.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The jealous companion's heartbeat is audible to the hero — not through the Mark, through proximity. It's racing. The hero can hear the effort the companion is making to control their breathing.
- **Smell**: Cortisol and adrenaline have a smell the Mark-enhanced hero can now detect. The jealous companion smells like the moments before combat. Their body is treating this emotion as a physical threat.
- **Touch**: If the hero touches the jealous companion, the Mark contracts briefly — then blazes. As though it's recalibrating. The hero feels a jolt of doubled emotion: their own concern and the companion's turmoil, layered over each other, briefly indistinguishable.
- **Sight**: The jealous companion's pupils are dilated. Not from low light — from arousal, rage, or both. The hero, with Mark-sharpened perception, can see the companion's pulse in their throat. It's too fast.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark treats jealousy as a bond intensifier. While the hero is near the distressed companion, the Mark is stronger than it's been since the first bond formed — warmer, brighter, more insistent. The hero may feel an inappropriate sense of wellbeing while comforting someone in pain. That dissonance — feeling good while someone they care about suffers because of them — is the core horror of the Mark's parasitism made visible for the first time. If the hero notices and is disturbed by their own reaction, good. That's the first step toward understanding what the Mark actually is.

## Beat 6: Rootbound Contact

**Goal**: Introduce the Rootbound, connect the Mark to the larger mystery, give the hero access to new knowledge

### What Happens
- A figure appears at the edge of the western forest. They've been watching Gorundtzal.
- Ayantu (or Dessalegn) makes contact — cautiously, on their terms
- They recognize the Mark. Their reaction is a mix of awe and fear. They call it by its old name.
- They offer help — but they want to understand the hero first

### Party Opportunities
- Talk to the Rootbound contact (what do they know about the Mark?)
- Visit the Rootbound Grove (the Mark responds to the channel node there)
- Learn about grounding rituals that can slow transformation
- Establish a relationship with the frontier communities

### Key Information Available
- The Mark is called "the Ember Tithe" — but they won't explain the full meaning yet
- The channels beneath Valduri are real and alive. The Rootbound tend them.
- The Communion's cauterization is killing the channels. This will kill the land.
- The Mark makes the hero important — and dangerous. They need to be careful.

### Tone
Mysterious, respectful, slightly unsettling. The Rootbound know something the hero doesn't, and they're not sure whether sharing it is wise.

### Horror Set Piece: The Channel Node
If the hero visits the Rootbound Grove, they're brought to a channel node — a place where the underground veins of the land surface briefly. It looks like a clearing where the tree roots have braided into a shallow basin. The basin holds no water but glows faintly amber from below. The Rootbound ask the hero to place their hand over the basin. The Mark ignites. Not warmth — ignition. The hero feels the entire network for one overwhelming second: miles of branching channels pulsing beneath the earth, alive, breathing, ancient. And threaded through the network, they feel their companions. Not nearby — bonded. The companions' heartbeats, their fears, their specific warmth. The hero can locate each one like stars in a constellation. Then the sensation breaks and the hero is on their knees, gasping, nose bleeding. The Rootbound are pale. "It's deeper than we thought," one says to the other. They didn't expect this. Whatever the Mark is doing, it's more advanced than they've seen before. Their offer of help suddenly carries a note of urgency it didn't have before.

### Intimate Scene Hook: The Walk Back
After the Rootbound contact, the hero must walk back to Gorundtzal. A companion accompanies them — chosen by the GM based on who the hero is closest to. The walk is long enough for a real conversation. The hero has just learned their Mark has a name, that the underground channels are real, that they are "important and dangerous." The companion sees the hero is shaken and does something simple: walks close enough that their shoulders touch. The hero can share what they learned or keep it private. Either choice has consequences — honesty deepens the bond (and the Mark), secrecy protects the companion but creates distance. The walk is beautiful: late afternoon light through frontier forest, the smell of pine and cold water, the sound of their boots on the path. A moment of peace that feels borrowed.

### Sensory Details
- **Sound**: The Rootbound grove is quiet in a way that isn't natural. No birdsong, no insect hum. The trees are alive and healthy, but the animal world gives the channel node a wide berth. When the Rootbound speak, their voices carry as though the clearing is acoustically shaped for conversation.
- **Smell**: The grove smells like living wood and turned earth — vital, rich, almost overwhelming. After the cauterizing substance's dead-nothing smell, this is its opposite: a density of scent that makes the hero's eyes water.
- **Touch**: The ground at the channel node vibrates. Not seismically — biologically. Like placing a hand on a sleeping animal's ribs. The hero's boots feel it first. Then their calves. Then their whole skeleton is humming.
- **Sight**: The amber glow from the channel node pulses. Not mechanically — organically. Like bioluminescence. It responds to the hero's proximity, brightening as they approach, and it matches the Mark's rhythm exactly. The Rootbound notice this and exchange a glance.

### The Mark Responds
The Mark is home here. That's the unsettling realization — the Mark behaves differently at the channel node, and the difference is comfort. The constant low-grade tension the hero carries (without realizing they carry it) drains away at the grove. The Mark settles into a deep, slow pulse. The hero feels rested, clear-headed, strong. Then they look at their companion and feel the bond thrum with a new clarity: they can sense the companion's emotional state, their physical wellbeing, their distance. Not vaguely — precisely. This is what the Mark wants. This is what it's building toward. The hero stands at the channel node and, for the first time, feels not cursed but chosen. The Rootbound see this on the hero's face and their fear deepens.

The hero noticed a bonded companion do something impossible during the fight and hasn't mentioned it. The companion hasn't mentioned it either. Both of them know.
   - **Mark status**: Active, sated. The crisis fed it. The hero may notice they feel better than they should — stronger, clearer, more alive — while the companion looks drawn and exhausted beyond what the fighting explains.

Next session, the hero must choose: seek the rituals (slowing transformation but potentially weakening bonds) or continue as they are. ## GM Notes

### The Romance-Horror Balance

After each beat, ask yourself three questions:
1. **Did I include at least one sensory detail of wrongness?** Not a jump scare, not a monster — a detail that registers as wrong. A shadow that doesn't match. A scent that shifted. A sound from inside a wall. If the answer is no, add one before moving on.
2. **Did the Mark respond to at least one emotional moment?** The Mark is the barometer. If the hero felt something real — connection, fear, tenderness, anger — the Mark should react. Warmth for bonding. Contraction for threat. Syncopation for transformation. Silence for decision. If the Mark was quiet all session, you missed opportunities.
3. **Did I create space for connection before I created danger?** Horror without intimacy is just threat. Intimacy without horror is just romance. The campaign lives in the intersection. Every dangerous moment should follow a vulnerable one. Every tender moment should carry a seed of dread.

### Per-Beat Balance Guide
- **Beats 1-2**: 70% intimacy, 30% wrongness. The horror is ambient. The connections are the focus.
- **Beat 3 (Takkele)**: 50/50. The information she brings is horrifying, but the decision about her creates emotional stakes.
- **Beat 4 (Psychological Shift)**: 60% horror, 40% intimacy. The horror IS intimate — it's happening to someone the hero cares about, and it's revealed in private moments.
- **Interludes**: 50/50. Each interlude should hit both registers equally. The Sleep-Walker is horror that creates intimacy. The Animal Reaction is intimacy disrupted by horror.
- **Beat 5 (External Threat)**: 40% horror, 60% intimacy. The threat itself is straightforward; the horror is in what the companions become during it. The aftermath is pure emotional space.
- **The Jealousy Incident**: 30% horror, 70% intimacy. This is primarily a relationship beat. The horror is subtext — the possessiveness isn't natural, and everyone involved suspects it.
- **Beat 6 (Rootbound Contact)**: 50/50. The channel node is awe-horror. The walk back is intimacy. The information creates dread.

### DO NOT
- **Do not rush physical transformation.** Sessions 1-3 are psychological only. No visible changes. No "her eyes looked different." The body comes later. Resist the urge to show instead of suggest.
- **Do not explain the Mark mechanically.** The hero should never think in game terms. Not "the Mark activated" but "warmth spread through my chest." Not "the bond strengthened" but "I couldn't stop thinking about her." The Mark is experienced as emotion, not as system.
- **Do not let the party feel safe for too long.** Two sessions of relative peace is the maximum. If the settlement is running smoothly and the companions are happy, something is wrong and the hero should know it. Comfort breeds complacency, and the Mark feeds on intensity.
- **Do not let the party feel unsafe for too long.** Constant threat numbs. If the last two beats were dangerous, the next should be quiet. The horror needs contrast. A peaceful morning that ends with the companion staring at their own hands, whispering "these don't feel like mine" — that's worse than any monster.
- **Do not let the hero off the hook.** The Mark responds to genuine emotion. The hero can't game it, can't fake bonds, can't optimize. Every real connection carries a real cost. Every moment of tenderness feeds the thing that will eventually consume the people they love. This should feel inescapable, not unfair — the hero isn't being punished for caring. They're being tithed.

### Pacing
- Act 1 should take 3-5 sessions. Don't rush the early bonds — they need to feel genuine for the later horror to land.
- Beats don't need to happen in strict order. The first bond (Beat 2) should happen naturally; the external threat (Beat 5) can come whenever the settlement needs shaking up.
- The Rootbound contact (Beat 6) should come AFTER the companion's psychological shift (Beat 4), so the hero has reason to seek answers.
- Interludes should be inserted at natural pauses — after a beat resolves and before the next begins. They're breather moments that deepen the dread.
- The Jealousy Incident requires 2+ bonds and should happen after enough time for both bonds to feel real. Rushing it makes the possessiveness seem arbitrary rather than earned.

### Transformation Tracking
- Keep a private log of transformation tendencies for each bonded companion
- Note which directions their choices and circumstances are pushing them
- Share nothing with players. Let it emerge through narrative.
- After each session, note: What did the companion do that was out of character? How did they handle stress? What environments did they spend time in? What emotions dominated their scenes? These notes shape transformation direction.

### The Mark's Behavior
- The Mark responds to genuine emotion, not proximity. Forced closeness doesn't feed it.
- When a bond deepens, describe warmth, rightness, a sense of the companion's presence even at distance
- When a companion begins to shift, the Mark may pulse differently near them — a subtle change the hero might notice
- The Mark has moods: warm (bonding), contracted (threat), syncopated (transformation), still (decision/waiting), blazing (crisis/harvest). Vary the vocabulary. Don't use "warm" for everything.

### Combat at Early Levels
- The party is fragile. Early combat should offer retreat options and non-lethal resolution paths.
- Gorundtzal's walls (once repaired) provide defensive advantages.
- Currodze's hidden weapon cache becomes available if trust is earned — this is a significant power boost.

### Character Spotlights
- Ensure every companion gets at least one meaningful scene in Act 1
- The first bond doesn't have to be romantic — it can be trust, respect, or shared purpose
- Track which companions the hero gravitates toward — this shapes transformation directions
- If one companion is dominating screen time, give another companion a crisis that demands attention. The hero's divided attention is itself a source of tension and the Mark notices which way they turn first.
