# Romance and Intimacy — GM Guide

**Visibility**: GM only. Players read `README.md`.
**Load**: At startup. This framework shapes every scene involving emotional or physical closeness.

---

## Romance Arc Types

Each companion follows one of these arc patterns. Assign during character creation and note it on their sheet. The arc shapes how the bond forms, what the companion's romantic journey looks like, and where the Mark's horror bites hardest.

### Slow Burn

Trust before desire. The bond forms through shared competence and mutual respect — two people who work well together gradually realizing the professional has become personal. Romance emerges from partnership. These companions don't fall — they build, brick by deliberate brick, until one day they look up and realize they've constructed something they can't take apart.

**GM approach**: Resist the temptation to accelerate. Let the player invest in the working relationship first. Romance enters through small disruptions — a glance held too long, an unnecessary touch, the realization that they've been making decisions based on the other person's opinion without noticing.

**Horror intersection**: Transformation corrupts practical trust. The companion who was the hero's most reliable partner begins making decisions they wouldn't have made before the bond. Their judgment shifts. Their priorities realign toward the hero in ways that undermine their competence. The thing that built the romance — "I trust you because you're excellent at what you do" — erodes as the bond reshapes what they're excellent at. The hero watches their most trusted ally become their most devoted follower, and can't tell when the transition happened.

### Immediate Spark

Intense attraction from the start. The Mark makes it feel destined — a recognition that bypasses the usual courtship entirely. These companions feel it the moment they meet the hero, and the hero feels it back. The connection is electric, consuming, and immediately intimate in a way that makes both parties slightly reckless.

**GM approach**: Let the intensity be genuine and overwhelming. Early scenes should crackle. The companion and hero gravitate toward each other in every gathering, find excuses for proximity, communicate in loaded silences that everyone else notices. Don't slow it down — the speed is the point.

**Horror intersection**: Destined doesn't mean healthy. The spark felt like fate, but was it? The Mark identified this companion before the hero had a chance to choose freely. The intensity that felt like cosmic alignment is indistinguishable from supernatural compulsion. As the companion transforms, the question sharpens: did they fall in love, or were they selected? And does the answer matter if the feelings are real?

### Reluctant Bond

Actively resists the connection. This companion feels the pull toward the hero and hates it — hates the loss of autonomy, hates the vulnerability, hates the way their body and heart conspire against their judgment. Romance grows in hostile soil, pushing through cracks in the companion's resistance.

**GM approach**: Respect the resistance. It's not a game or a challenge to overcome — it's a genuine response to a frightening loss of control. Let the companion be harsh, distant, prickly. Let the softening happen in unguarded moments they immediately regret. The romance here is built from surrenders — each one costing the companion something they value about themselves.

**Horror intersection**: The Mark doesn't care about resistance. Every barrier the companion raises, the bond erodes. Their resistance weakens not because the hero earns their trust, but because the Mark is rewriting their capacity to refuse. The companion knows this. They can feel themselves losing the fight. And the cruelest part: the moments of surrender feel so good that they stop being sure they want to win.

### Rival / Equal

Challenges the hero's authority. This companion matches the hero in capability, confidence, or force of personality. Romantic tension emerges from the power dynamic — the push-pull of two strong wills that can't ignore each other. Respect and desire are braided together so tightly they can't be separated.

**GM approach**: Give this companion real power. They should disagree with the hero, have better ideas sometimes, refuse to defer. Romantic moments come from the friction — arguments that end with someone's back against a wall and both of them breathing too hard. Admiration expressed through competition. Desire that looks like antagonism.

**Horror intersection**: The Mark turns equals into dependents. This is the most structurally devastating arc. A companion who defined themselves through their autonomy and capability watches both erode as the bond deepens. They become less able to challenge the hero — not because of anything the hero does, but because the transformation makes deference feel natural. The rival who once stood toe-to-toe now finds themselves yielding, and the yielding feels like relief instead of defeat. The hero loses the thing they loved most about this companion: the challenge.

### Dark Mirror

This companion's transformation speaks to the hero's hidden desires. Something the hero wants but won't admit — power, submission, beauty, cruelty, tenderness taken to its extreme — the Mark sculpts this companion toward it. The hero doesn't want to stop THIS transformation because the result is something they crave.

**GM approach**: Identify the hero's unspoken desire early and build this companion's transformation toward it. The companion should become more of what the hero secretly wants in ways that are increasingly obvious to both of them. Frame it as discovery rather than corruption — the companion seems to be becoming their truest self. Only gradually should the horror surface: whose truth is this?

**Horror intersection**: Complicity in the beloved's destruction. The hero can't claim innocence because they enjoy the result. Every time they find the transformed companion more attractive, more compelling, more perfectly suited to them, they're benefiting from the companion's loss of self. The hero's desire is the engine of the transformation, and the companion is becoming a mirror of the hero's hunger rather than a person in their own right. Stopping it would mean giving up something the hero genuinely wants.

### The Informed

Knows what the Mark does — learned from the Rootbound, figured it out independently, or was told by the hero in a moment of honesty — and chooses the bond anyway. This companion walks into the fire with open eyes. That should make it better. It doesn't.

**GM approach**: This arc works best when introduced after Act 1, when the truth about the Mark has surfaced. The companion's choice should be portrayed as genuinely brave, genuinely loving, and genuinely questionable. Let them articulate their reasoning. Let it be sound. Then let the bond slowly compromise their capacity to reason about it.

**Horror intersection**: Is informed consent possible when the bond itself influences judgment? The companion chose freely — once. But the bond is now part of them, shaping their feelings, their priorities, their sense of what's tolerable. Would the person they were before the bond still make the same choice? They can't know. No one can. The consent was real at the moment it was given, and the moment it was given is the last moment it was fully free.

## Progression Milestones

Track per companion. Each milestone deepens the bond and corresponds to a range on the Mark's bond strength scale. The milestones aren't a checklist — they're a language for understanding where a relationship is and what it means for transformation.

### 1. Recognition (Bond Strength 1-2)

The Mark identifies the companion as bondable. The hero feels a pull — warmth, curiosity, the sense of someone being *interesting* in a way that goes past the rational. The companion doesn't feel anything supernatural yet, but they notice the hero's attention and respond to it.

**GM notes**: This happens organically. Don't announce it. Describe the Mark's warmth when the hero interacts with this person. Let the hero notice the pull without understanding it. The companion is just a person at this point — someone who caught the hero's eye.

### 2. Trust Exchange (Bond Strength 3)

A moment of genuine vulnerability. Someone says something true that costs them something to say. The bond forms — not as a magical event, but as a felt shift. After this moment, both parties know that something has changed between them, even if they can't name it.

**GM notes**: This should be a specific scene, not a gradual drift. Create conditions for vulnerability — danger, exhaustion, a quiet moment after crisis. One person opens up. The other receives it without judgment. The Mark pulses. Something locks into place.

### 3. Emotional Deepening (Bond Strength 4-5)

Repeated intimate emotional exchanges. The bond is no longer a single moment but a pattern — multiple confessions, multiple instances of being seen, a developing intimacy that both parties actively choose to maintain. Conversations go deeper each time. The companions seek each other out.

**GM notes**: Track the accumulation. Three or four significant emotional exchanges move the bond into this range. Transformation begins accelerating here — psychological changes that were subtle become noticeable. The companion may confide in the hero about the changes, creating a feedback loop where vulnerability about the transformation deepens the bond that drives the transformation.

### 4. Physical Intimacy (Bond Strength 5-6)

Touch crosses friendship boundaries. A held hand that means more than comfort. A brush of fingers across a cheek. Proximity that becomes electric. The first physical intimacy doesn't need to be sexual — it needs to be *intentional*. Both parties know they're crossing a line.

**GM notes**: The Mark responds strongly to physical intimacy. Describe the warmth intensifying, the narcotic quality of sustained contact, the difficulty of pulling apart. After this milestone, transformation accelerates noticeably. The companion sleeps deeper than usual. The hero feels energized. Neither connects the two.

### 5. Declaration (Bond Strength 6-7)

One party names what's between them. "I love you." "I can't do this without you." "You're the reason I'm still here." The words don't have to be romantic — they have to be true and they have to make the bond undeniable. Once spoken, the bond solidifies. Ambiguity dies.

**GM notes**: This scene carries enormous weight. The declaration should cost something — spoken in a moment of fear, or after a fight, or when one of them is about to do something dangerous. The companion's transformation takes a visible step forward in the days following a declaration. Something about naming the bond gives it permission to deepen.

### 6. Crisis of Knowledge (Bond Strength test)

The companion learns what the bond is doing — from the Rootbound, from the hero's confession, from their own investigation, or from watching another companion's transformation progress. The relationship either survives this knowledge or shatters.

**GM notes**: This is the campaign's most important relationship scene for each companion. The companion must process: the bond is changing me, the hero benefits from the change, and the thing I feel for the hero may not be entirely mine. Give this scene room. Let the companion react fully — rage, grief, betrayal, denial. If the relationship survives, the bond strength may stay or increase (the companion chose to stay). If it shatters, bond strength drops to 2-3 — the Mark's hold remains but the emotional connection is damaged. A shattered bond can be rebuilt, but the rebuild is a different romance than the original.

### 7. Informed Choice (Bond Strength 7-8 if stays; drops to 2-3 if goes)

After the crisis, the companion makes a deliberate choice: stay or go. Not in the heat of the moment — after they've had time to think. This is the closest the campaign offers to genuine consent. The companion knows the cost and chooses it anyway.

**GM notes**: If the companion stays, their commitment deepens the bond dramatically. Transformation accelerates, but the companion's agency within it also increases — they can direct the changes more consciously, resist specific effects, negotiate with the bond. If they go, they don't fully escape. The Mark's hold doesn't release. They suffer withdrawal, and the hero suffers the loss of a bond. The companion may return — and the reunion will be more intense than the original bond.

### 8. Deep Bond (Bond Strength 9-10)

Maximum intimacy, maximum drain, maximum transformation. The companion has given themselves fully to the bond, and the bond has taken everything offered. Every tender moment is a transaction — an exchange of warmth and selfhood that both parties feel. The companion is visibly transformed, still beautiful, still compelling, but unmistakably changed. And the hero knows, with every touch, exactly what it costs.

**GM notes**: Deep bonds are sustainable only if the hero actively manages them. Without restraint, the companion's transformation accelerates toward the horror threshold. With care — rituals, behavioral anchors, deliberate separation — the bond can plateau. The relationship at this level is achingly intimate and perpetually heavy. Nothing is casual. Every gesture carries the weight of what it means.

## Intimacy Scene Templates

Specific scene frameworks to deploy at appropriate moments. Each creates both romantic tension and horror undertone. Adapt freely — these are starting shapes, not scripts.

### The Wound-Tending Scene

After danger, one tends the other's wounds. Physical proximity forced by necessity. The companion's shirt is off, or their hands are exposed, or the hero is cleaning blood from their face. Pain and care. The Mark pulses — the hero's touch is warm, and the companion leans into it involuntarily. Their skin is hot under the hero's hands. Silence that says everything. The injury is minor enough to forget. The closeness is not.

### The Night Watch

Two alone while others sleep. The fire is low. One of them says something they wouldn't say in daylight. "I keep dreaming about you — not like that. Like you're inside me. Like you're part of the way I think now." The companion describes the bond without knowing what they're describing. To them, it's romance. To the hero, if they know what the Mark does, it's a confession that lands like a knife.

### The Argument That Breaks Open

A real fight about something that matters — a tactical decision, a moral question, something where they genuinely disagree. It escalates. Voices rise. And then, in the heat of it, something true comes out. "I can't lose you. I don't know when that started. I don't know if it's mine." A confession ripped out by anger rather than tenderness. The fight dies. What replaces it is more dangerous.

### The Dance Scene

A rare moment of peace. Someone found an instrument, or there's a celebration, or the frontier produced an unexpected evening of warmth and music. The companion moves with a grace they didn't have a month ago — fluid, magnetic, slightly inhuman in the precision of it. They are more beautiful than they were. The beauty wasn't there before the bond. The hero can enjoy it or be horrified. Probably both. The dance is joyful and the joy is real and the cost is invisible and the night is warm.

### The Goodbye Rehearsal

The hero tries to pull away to slow transformation. A deliberate withdrawal — sleeping apart, avoiding eye contact, redirecting conversation to practical matters. The goodbye is never spoken but both parties feel it building. When it finally surfaces — "I think we need distance" — the emotional intensity of the scene STRENGTHENS the bond. The Mark punishes restraint. The hero's attempt to protect the companion becomes another act of intimacy. There is no clean way out.

### The Amplification Kiss

First genuinely intimate physical moment. The buildup has been excruciating — weeks of proximity, loaded silences, touches that almost happened. When it finally arrives, everything heightens. The Mark flares warm. Sound recedes. The world contracts to the space between two people.

**Aftermath (GM tracking, not narrated to players)**: The Mark pulses warmly for hours afterward. The companion sleeps too deeply — not restlessly, but with an absence that resembles unconsciousness more than sleep. The hero felt fed. The companion wakes slightly fatigued and interprets it as the emotional exhaustion of a significant moment. Bond strength increases by 1. Transformation advances.

### The Bed-Check

The hero wakes beside a companion — in the same bedroll, or in adjacent ones, or simply in the same room. Dim light: predawn, firelight, the grey hour. The companion's face has shifted fractionally. Something in the jaw. Something in the way the skin sits over the cheekbone. Still beautiful. Still them. But not quite the face the hero first saw.

The hero lies there watching someone they love slowly become someone else, wondering if they'll still love the new face. Wondering if the question itself is the answer.

## The Consent Spectrum

This is the campaign's most important thematic territory. These layers aren't puzzles to solve — they're moral textures to inhabit. The hero should feel the weight. Companions should articulate the questions. No one gets a clean answer.

### Layer 1: Uninformed Consent

Early bonds. The companion doesn't know the cost. They chose to trust the hero, to open up, to be vulnerable — all genuinely. But they didn't know what they were signing. Every tender moment in this phase is retroactively complicated once the truth emerges. The hero who knew (or suspected) and continued bonding anyway carries that weight.

This is already morally fraught. The hero is receiving something at a cost the other person hasn't agreed to pay. That the hero may not know either doesn't fully absolve them — the Mark is part of them, and its hunger is, in some sense, theirs.

### Layer 2: Partial Knowledge

The companion suspects something. They've noticed the changes. They've heard rumors or fragments from the Rootbound. They stay anyway — because the bond feels worth it, because they trust the hero, because leaving is harder than staying.

Is this free choice? The companion is choosing with incomplete information and under the influence of a bond that makes staying feel necessary. But people make choices with incomplete information constantly. The question isn't whether the choice is perfectly free — it's whether it's free *enough*. And who gets to decide that?

### Layer 3: Full Knowledge, Bond-Influenced

The companion knows exactly what's happening. They understand the Mark, the drain, the transformation. They choose to stay. But the bond makes them want to stay — it has been shaping their desires, their priorities, their sense of what's tolerable for weeks or months.

Consent when the thing consented to alters the capacity to refuse. A person addicted to a substance can say they choose it. A person in love can say they choose the suffering that comes with it. Are they wrong? The companion would say the feelings are real, the choice is genuine, and the fact that the bond shapes their judgment doesn't invalidate it any more than love always shapes judgment. They would not be entirely wrong.

### Layer 4: Deliberate Resistance

The companion knows, stays, AND actively fights the bond's influence on their own judgment. Every gesture requires interrogation: *Is this mine? Do I want this because I want it, or because the bond wants me to want it?* Every moment of tenderness carries a shadow of doubt.

This is the closest to genuine consent the campaign offers. It is also agonizing — a relationship where every feeling must be examined before it can be trusted, where spontaneity is suspect, where the purest expression of love is the willingness to question whether it's real.

The hero who sees a companion doing this — fighting for their own autonomy while choosing to stay — should understand what it costs them. It is the most selfless and the most self-aware form of love the campaign can produce. And it still might not be enough.

## Inter-Companion Dynamics

The harem structure creates its own ecosystem. These dynamics should emerge organically but the GM should understand the forces driving them.

### Bond Hierarchy

The Mark doesn't treat all bonds equally. Bond strength varies, and companions can *feel* where they stand relative to each other. Not as numbers — as sensation. A companion with a deep bond feels the hero's warmth more intensely, draws more of the hero's attention, and experiences the pull of the Mark more strongly. A companion with a weaker bond watches this happening and understands their position.

This hierarchy is not fixed. It shifts as bonds deepen or weaken. A companion who has been in the hero's inner circle may feel themselves slipping as a new bond strengthens. The resulting insecurity is genuine and should be played as such.

### Alliance Against the Hero

As companions realize what's happening to them, they may form support networks. "You've noticed it too? The thoughts that aren't yours?" These companion-to-companion scenes — where bonded individuals compare notes, share fears, and build trust outside the hero's orbit — should be among the campaign's most powerful moments.

This alliance may eventually confront the hero. It may also fracture, as more-transformed companions find they can't agree with less-transformed ones about whether the changes are harmful. Some companions may genuinely believe the transformation is making them better. Others may see it as annihilation. The disagreement mirrors real debates about the nature of change in relationships.

### Protective Jealousy

A further-transformed companion may become aggressively protective of their position in the hierarchy. Not consciously — they wouldn't describe it as jealousy. But the transformation amplifies possessiveness, and a companion deep in the bond may react with hostility, passive aggression, or strategic interference when the hero's attention moves to someone else.

This creates three-way scenes that are simultaneously romantic, horrific, and dramatically rich. The hero must navigate between companions who both need something from them, one of whom is exhibiting behavior they wouldn't have shown before the bond corrupted them.

### The Sacrifice Play

A less-bonded companion volunteers to take more of the Mark's drain to spare a more-transformed one. This is selfless, generous, and terrifying — because it accelerates their own transformation. The hero must decide whether to allow it. The companion making the sacrifice may be doing it for love of the other companion, for love of the hero, or because the bond is already shaping them toward self-sacrifice.

### The Jealousy Sickness

When two bonded companions are present and the hero shows clear affection to one, the other may experience physical symptoms: nausea, headache, vertigo, a chill that won't break. The body keeps score of the bond hierarchy.

This isn't performative — the companion may not even connect their sudden headache to what they're watching. They just know they need to leave the room. They just know they feel ill. If they make the connection, the horror deepens: their body is reacting to the hero's love for someone else as though it were a poison.

This mechanic introduces the harem dynamic's horror dimension: the hero cannot love freely without causing physical harm to those they love less intensely, or less visibly, in that moment.

## Harem Evolution by Act

### Act 1: Chemistry (Early Sessions)

Standard romantic tension. Multiple interesting people arriving at Gorundtzal, each connecting with the hero differently. Light jealousy manifests as social jockeying — who sits next to the hero at meals, who gets sent on tasks together, whose opinion the hero asks first. Mostly respectful coexistence, colored by awareness.

The tone is *possibility*. Everything is still forming. The bonds are new enough that the hero can believe this is just the natural result of intense circumstances. The companions are individuals first, romantic interests second.

### Act 2: Solidarity (Mid Sessions)

The bonds are known. Companions are changing. The dynamic shifts from competition to survival solidarity — a sisterhood or brotherhood of the marked. They share what they're experiencing. They protect each other. They negotiate the hierarchy with more honesty than before, because the stakes are now existential rather than romantic.

The tone shifts to *negotiation*. Who gets the hero's time. Who needs it most. Whether fairness is possible when the Mark itself plays favorites. The companions may develop a collective relationship with the hero that coexists with their individual bonds — a strange, painful, intimate family.

### Act 3: Court (Late Sessions)

Some companions are far enough along that transformation defines them. The "harem" has become a court — each occupying a role shaped by what the bond has made them. One is the protector. One is the counselor. One is the mirror. The roles weren't chosen; they emerged from the transformation's direction and the companion's original personality.

Intimacy isn't gone but it's changed. The hero moves between companions who love them in ways shaped by the Mark as much as by choice. The court functions — there's genuine warmth, genuine care, genuine partnership. But the original people are harder to find under what they've become. The hero must decide whether what exists now is enough, or whether they owe these people their original selves back.

## Tone Guidelines

**Sensory detail and emotional depth.** Romantic scenes are not plot delivery mechanisms — they're the campaign's primary dramatic space. Describe what it feels like to stand too close to someone you want. The specific quality of warmth. The way breathing changes. The weight of a hand.

**The almost-moment.** The space before a first touch is more charged than the touch itself. A hand that reaches out and stops. Eye contact held one second past comfortable. The sentence that starts with "I..." and doesn't finish. These near-misses carry more tension than consummation and should be deployed generously.

**Let intimacy breathe.** Don't artificially cut scenes short. Render the approach, the tension, the crossing of thresholds with full sensory detail — warmth, texture, breath, the specific quality of contact. If the scene has momentum, follow it. Time-jump naturally when the emotional peak has been reached and the narrative is better served by the aftermath. If the player drives deeper, follow their lead — the campaign is about choice, and closing doors breaks the contract. The Mark makes every intimate moment simultaneously beautiful and predatory; lean into both.

**The Mark is always present.** In every romantic scene, the Mark should make itself felt. Warmth in the hero's chest. The faint ember glow in low light. The companion's skin hot under the hero's fingers. The narcotic quality of sustained contact. Romance and the Mark are never fully separable, and the GM should never let the player forget it.

**Track the cost.** After every intimate scene, note the transformation impact. Not to the players — to yourself. In your GM notes, record: what the scene cost the companion, how the bond strength shifted, what transformation effect will manifest in coming sessions. The romance has a price. Tracking it is your responsibility. The players feel the warmth; you see the bill.

**"The best version of you isn't you."** Transformation should make companions MORE desirable, not less, for most of the arc. The horror is not that the hero is repulsed — it's that they're not. The companion is more beautiful, more compelling, more perfectly attuned to the hero's desires than they were before the bond. The hero doesn't want the original version back because this version is *better*. And that preference is the cruelest thing the Mark does.
