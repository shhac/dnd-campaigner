# Life at Gorundtzal

## What You See

Gorundtzal is a waystation — or was. Stone walls, six feet high, built to stop bandits and bad weather. A gate that still closes if you lean into it. A well that draws cold, iron-tinged water from somewhere deep. The bones of a market square, an inn called the Tallow Post with three walls and most of a roof, stables that smell like horses that aren't there anymore.

A Communion patrol came through ten days before you arrived. They took what they wanted and burned what they didn't. The south wall has a fifteen-foot gap where they broke through. The upper floor of the inn is open sky. Half the merchant buildings around the square are shells.

About thirty people are here. Refugees, mostly — fled from towns further south when the Communion swept through, or wandered north because there was nowhere else to go. A few frontier folk who were already close. No one is in charge. A former soldier named Currodze has taken it upon herself to ration the remaining supplies, which makes her the closest thing to authority anyone has.

People sleep in the inn's ground floor, in the stables, under lean-tos propped against the remaining walls. Cook fires in the square at dusk. Conversation stays quiet. Everyone is waiting to see if this place holds together or falls apart.

## What Needs Doing

You don't need anyone to tell you. Walk the perimeter and it's obvious.

**The wall gap.** Fifteen feet of open ground on the south side. Anything — wolves, bandits, a Communion patrol — walks right in. Fixing it means timber, stone, and enough hands to do the lifting. It's the single most important thing that could happen here.

**The well.** It works, but the mechanism groans. The rope is fraying. If the well fails, Gorundtzal is finished. Someone who knows rope and ironwork should look at it before it becomes an emergency.

**Shelter.** Thirty people and not enough roofs. The stables work but they're miserable. The inn holds maybe a dozen comfortably. Everyone else is exposed to wind and weather. Patching roofs, hanging tarps, sealing gaps — it's unglamorous work but it's the difference between surviving winter and not.

**Food.** Currodze's supplies won't last. The frontier forests have game — boar, elk, hares — but hunting takes people away from the walls and makes noise that carries. Foraging is safer but slower. A proper arrangement, hunting parties with someone who knows tracking, would go further than individuals wandering out with improvised weapons.

**Organization.** Thirty strangers with no structure. Some want to help. Some are too broken to do much. A few are already grumbling about who gets what. Someone needs to give people purpose — assign tasks, settle disputes, make decisions when there's no good option. That person will earn trust. They'll also earn blame.

## How Things Get Better

Nothing happens by announcement. You don't declare "we're repairing the wall" and it appears. You need people willing to work, materials to work with, and time to do it.

**Labor.** Most projects need hands — ten, fifteen, twenty people depending on the scope. Not everyone here is able-bodied. Some are injured, some are elderly, some are children. The people who can work have limits. Push them too hard without enough food and morale collapses. Organize them well and they'll surprise you.

**Materials.** The frontier has timber. The ruined buildings have salvageable stone. Metal is scarce — nails, hinges, tools are worth more than coin right now. Trade, scavenging, and creative reuse are how you get what you need. The trader Pellinaste has salvaged goods and knows what's available in the region, for a price.

**Time.** A wall doesn't go up in a day. Most improvements take days or weeks of sustained effort. Some — like establishing hunting parties or trade connections — are ongoing commitments that pay off slowly. You'll be balancing immediate survival against longer-term investment constantly.

**Trust.** People work harder when they believe in what they're building. A settlement where people feel heard, where disputes get resolved fairly, where someone has a plan — that settlement builds faster than one held together by fear and proximity.

## The People

These aren't your people. Not yet. They're strangers thrown together by catastrophe.

Some came from Kethenvari — city folk, educated, used to structure and comfort, now sleeping in a stable. Some are frontier settlers whose homesteads were raided. A few are former soldiers from scattered Valduri units. They carry different skills, different expectations, and different griefs.

There's no shared identity here beyond survival. The city folk look down on the frontier settlers. The frontier settlers resent the city folk's helplessness. The soldiers are used to command structures that no longer exist. Everyone has lost someone or something. Some are ready to build. Some just want to stop running.

Whoever steps up to lead — by action, not by title — will have to navigate all of this. Every decision about resources, shelter, and priority is also a decision about who matters and who doesn't.

## Winter

It's late autumn. You can feel it in the mornings — frost on the stone, breath visible at dawn. The frontier winters are hard. Deep snow, cutting wind, temperatures that kill the unprepared.

First frost is days away, maybe a week. First real snow within the month. By deep winter, the world outside these walls will be hostile to travel, hostile to hunting, hostile to anything but huddling close to a fire and hoping the supplies hold.

What that means practically:
- **Shelter repairs can't wait.** Every week of delay is a week closer to people freezing.
- **Food stores need building now.** Hunting is harder in snow. Foraging is impossible. What you stockpile before winter is what you have.
- **The cold is also protection.** Communion patrols thin out in winter. They retreat to garrisons. The worst months for survival are also the safest from occupation.
- **Spring changes everything.** When the thaw comes, travel resumes, refugees arrive in numbers, and the Communion starts moving again. What you've built by then determines whether Gorundtzal is ready.

The race isn't against the Communion. Not yet. The race is against the weather.
