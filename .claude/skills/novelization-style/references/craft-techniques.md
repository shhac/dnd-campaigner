# Literary Fantasy Craft Techniques

Advanced prose reference for literary dark fantasy. Load when you need deep guidance on specific techniques. Drawn from Le Guin, Hobb, Clarke, Miller, and workshop craft tradition.

## Psychic Distance Spectrum

Control the narrative gap between reader and character's internal life.

| Level | Name | Technique | Example |
|-------|------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Fully External | Observable behavior only; reader infers emotion | A character knocks away a hand helping wipe gravy from their chin |
| 2 | Subjective External | Narrator interprets from outside: "seemed," "appeared" | "In the intensity of her look there was a touch of dread" |
| 3 | Reported Interiority | Thought tags: "he thought," "she realized" | "Perhaps, he thought, he would have a glass of araq" |
| 4 | Full Immersion (FID) | Character's voice permeates narration itself | Using "stupid" repeatedly reflects the character's judgment, not the narrator's |

**Controls:**
- "Watched" is external; "saw" suggests character perception
- Character vocabulary appearing in narration signals closeness
- Mental action verbs ("realized," "wondered") signal Level 3, not Level 4
- "As though" / "seemed" maintain external distance while suggesting inner states

**Principle:** Close narration almost always feels like showing. Reporting feels like telling. Stay at one level per beat — zoom in for emotional peaks, out for transitions.

### Free Indirect Discourse (FID)

Embed character thoughts within narration without quotation marks or tags.

**Instead of:** "Hal realized something was different; something had changed."
**Use:** "Something was different; something had changed."

**Tense signals:**
- Direct thought (present, italicized): *What is it?*
- FID narration (past, untagged): What was it?

FID doesn't mean every sentence must use it. The narrator's voice handles transitions and description; intense moments slip into FID.

## The Maass Emotional Showing Technique

To create genuine emotional complexity without sentimentality:

1. Identify the character's primary feeling
2. Ask what else they're feeling (repeat 3 times to find depth)
3. Objectify the emotion — create an analogy or image for it
4. Have the character make a moral judgment about their own feeling
5. Add sensory details unique to how this specific character perceives this specific scene
6. **Delete all the named emotions. Keep only the secondary information.**

The reader feels what the character feels through behavior and perception, not through being told.

## Dialogue as Iceberg (Hemingway)

Three layers:
- **The said**: Explicit words on the page
- **The unsaid**: What characters deliberately withhold from each other
- **The unsayable**: What characters won't admit even to themselves

**Craft principles:**
- Every line must escalate conflict, unmask desire, or corner characters into irrevocable decisions
- Silence as response: what's withheld often carries more weight than what's spoken
- Repetition signals insistence — a character who repeats a phrase is trying to convince themselves
- Exposition as ammunition: characters reveal backstory to wound, justify, or manipulate — never to inform the reader
- Characters talk around what matters — deflection, humor, subject changes, over-specificity about mundane things

## Worldbuilding Through Incluing

Jo Walton's term: "scattering information seamlessly through the text, as opposed to stopping the story to impart it."

**Five methods:**
1. **Dialogue** — Characters discuss things they have reason to discuss now. Avoid "idiot lectures" where characters explain things both already know.
2. **Environment interaction** — Casual competence with something alien to the reader reveals the world without pausing to explain.
3. **Internal thought** — What the POV character takes for granted tells the reader what's normal. What surprises them tells the reader what's unusual.
4. **Background detail** — Specific cultural markers woven into scene texture.
5. **Memory** — Brief surfacing of past that illuminates present world-state while revealing character.

**Clarke method**: Voice itself as worldbuilding. When narration sounds like it belongs to the world, the reader absorbs setting through every sentence.

**Miller method**: The more distant your world from the reader's reality, the sharper your sensory anchors must be. Pair the strange with the mundane.

**Rules of restraint:**
- Only include worldbuilding that serves the scene's emotional or narrative purpose
- Introduce information only when a character needs or encounters it
- Raise questions before answering them
- If a passage is mostly worldbuilding with no character movement, cut it

## Tension in Quiet Scenes

- **Anchor in desire**: What does the character yearn for in this moment?
- **Sensory sharpening**: When external action slows, internal and sensory detail must sharpen
- **Subtext over surface**: Let conflict simmer through what remains unsaid
- **Symbolic settings**: Backdrops echoing the character's internal landscape
- **Micro-tension**: Every gesture carries emotional charge, but something must shift — stillness is not stagnation

## Prose Rhythm (Le Guin)

"The sound of the language is where it all begins."

- Short, choppy sentences: tension, urgency, shock
- Long, flowing sentences: contemplation, ease, expansiveness
- The shift between them is itself the tool
- Read your prose aloud — dull, jerky, or feeble prose becomes obvious when vocalized

### Sonic Texture
- Hard consonants (k, t, p) for tension and violence
- Sibilants (s, sh) for unease or seduction
- Open vowels for grief or wonder
- Germanic-root words for rawness and physicality
- Latinate vocabulary for abstraction, ceremony, authority

### The Narrative Sentence
Le Guin: "The chief duty of a narrative sentence is to lead to the next sentence." Every sentence must create enough momentum or curiosity to pull the reader forward — through plot, character, atmosphere, or rhythm.

## Character Interiority (Hobb)

"Characters are shaped by their experiences. We become the product of what has been done to us."

**Method**: Put the character on like donning a coat. Think and believe as they do. Let them react as they truly would — even when it creates narrative complications.

**Techniques:**
1. Dig beneath physical reactions — the psychological reason underneath is the interesting part
2. Show processing, not just outcomes — reveal how characters make sense of events as they unfold
3. Use subjective impressions — layer character perspective into environmental description
4. Context-shifting (Fonda Lee): characters present differently depending on relationships and environment

## What Distinguishes Literary from Genre Fantasy

- **Voice**: Every sentence is deliberate craft, not just plot delivery
- **Thematic ambiguity**: Characters contain contradictions; the world's problems don't have tidy solutions
- **Earned emotional moments**: Patient buildup, not dramatic shortcuts
- **Interiority as engine**: The story advances through how characters process events, not just events themselves
- **Restraint**: Resist explaining what can be implied, resolving what can remain ambiguous, showing the monster when evidence of the monster is more frightening
